Lab 055 - OSPF (Part 2: Multi-Area)

==================================================
LAB OBJECTIVE
==================================================

Expand the single-area OSPF topology from Lab 054 (OSPF Part 1) into a
five-router, three-area hierarchical OSPF design: Area 0 (the backbone,
which every other area must connect through), Area 1, and Area 2, with
R2 and R3 acting as ABRs (Area Border Routers — routers with interfaces
in more than one area). Configure OSPF correctly per-area on all five
routers, then configure route summarization on both ABRs to reduce the
size of the routing tables in the non-backbone areas.

==================================================
TOPOLOGY
==================================================

    AREA 1                          AREA 0 (BACKBONE)              AREA 2

  +--------+                                                    +--------+
  |   R4   |  RID 4.4.4.4                                       |   R5   |  RID 5.5.5.5
  +---+----+                                                    +---+----+
      |                                                              |
      | 10.14.0.0/24                                    10.35.0.0/24 |
      |                                                              |
  +---+----+   10.12.0.0/24   +--------+   10.23.0.0/24   +---------+
  |   R1   |------------------|   R2   |------------------|   R3    |
  +--------+                  +--------+                  +---------+
  RID 1.1.1.1                 RID 2.2.2.2                 RID 3.3.3.3
  (all-area1)                 ABR: area1 + area0           ABR: area0 + area2
                               loopback in area0            loopback in area0

R1 sits at the center of Area 1, connected to both R4 and R2. R2 is an
ABR — its R1-facing interface is in Area 1, its R3-facing interface is
in Area 0, and (per this lab's instructions) its loopback is placed in
Area 0. R3 is the second ABR — its R2-facing interface is in Area 0,
its R5-facing interface is in Area 2, and its loopback is also placed
in Area 0. R4 and R5 are each entirely within their own single area
(Area 1 and Area 2 respectively), including their own loopbacks. Every
non-backbone area (1 and 2) connects to the network only through Area
0 — there is no direct Area 1-to-Area 2 link, by OSPF design
requirement.

STEP 1 - CONFIGURE MULTI-AREA OSPF ON ALL FIVE ROUTERS
==================================================

---------------- R1 (entirely within Area 1) ----------------

Command                                    Explanation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
enable
configure terminal
router ospf 1                               Same process ID (1) used on every
                                            router in this lab — a deliberate
                                            contrast with Lab 054, where a
                                            different process ID was used per
                                            router specifically to prove it's only
                                            locally significant; here the more
                                            typical real-world convention (same
                                            process ID everywhere) is used instead.
 network 0.0.0.0 255.255.255.255 area 1      Since every one of R1's interfaces
                                            belongs to Area 1, this catch-all
                                            shortcut activates OSPF on all of them
                                            at once, entirely within Area 1.
 passive-interface Loopback0                 Suppresses OSPF hellos on the
                                            loopback (still advertised, just not
                                            sending/receiving hello traffic).
 auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000        Sets the reference bandwidth to
                                            100,000 Mbps (100 Gbps), per this
                                            lab's instruction that a 100 Gigabit
                                            interface should compute to a cost of
                                            1 — must match on every router.

---------------- R2 (ABR: Area 1 + Area 0) ----------------

Command                                    Explanation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
enable
configure terminal
router ospf 1
 network 10.12.0.0 0.0.0.255 area 1          The R1-facing link, in Area 1. A /24
                                            wildcard was used here (matching the
                                            interface's actual mask), though a
                                            broader /16 wildcard would also have
                                            worked — a /8 wildcard would NOT,
                                            since 10.0.0.0/8 would also match
                                            R2's other interface (the R3-facing
                                            one), which needs to be in a
                                            different area.
 network 10.23.0.0 0.0.0.255 area 0          The R3-facing link, in Area 0 (the
                                            backbone) — this is what makes R2 an
                                            ABR: it has interfaces in two
                                            different areas.
 network 2.2.2.2 0.0.0.0 area 0              The loopback, placed in Area 0 per
                                            this lab's specific instruction (the
                                            lab notes that for R2 and R3, the
                                            loopback could technically go in
                                            either area they touch, but Area 0 was
                                            chosen).
 passive-interface Loopback0
 auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000

==================================================
STEP 2 - CONFIGURE ROUTE SUMMARIZATION ON BOTH ABRs
==================================================

Before summarization, R5's routing table contains individual routes to
every 10.x.0.0/24 subnet in the network — 10.12.0.0/24, 10.14.0.0/24,
and 10.23.0.0/24 — even though R5 only has a single path (through R3)
to reach any of them. In a small lab this is harmless, but in a large
network, replacing many specific routes with one summary route
meaningfully reduces routing table size and OSPF's computational load.

---------------- R3 ----------------

Command                                    Explanation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
router ospf 1
 area 0 range 10.0.0.0 255.0.0.0            Summarizes every 10.x.x.x route R3
                                            currently sees associated with Area 0
                                            (10.12.0.0/24 and 10.14.0.0/24, which
                                            actually originate in Area 1 but
                                            arrive at R3 as Area 0 inter-area
                                            routes via R2, plus 10.23.0.0/24,
                                            native to Area 0 itself) into a
                                            single 10.0.0.0/8 route, before
                                            advertising it onward into Area 2.
                                            The mask given here (255.0.0.0) is a
                                            REGULAR subnet mask, not a wildcard
                                            mask — easy to mix up with the
                                            `network` command's wildcard syntax
                                            used earlier in this lab.

Loopback addresses (1.1.1.1/32, 2.2.2.2/32, etc.) fall outside the
10.0.0.0/8 range and are therefore unaffected — they continue to be
advertised individually.

---------------- Verify (R5) ----------------

Command                Result
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
show ip route            Instead of separate routes to 10.12.0.0/24, 10.14.0.0/24,
                         and 10.23.0.0/24, R5 now has a single summarized
                         10.0.0.0/8 route learned via OSPF. R5's own directly
                         connected 10.35.0.0/24 network is still listed
                         separately, as expected (a directly connected network is
                         never replaced by a learned summary).

---------------- R2 ----------------

Command                                    Explanation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
router ospf 1
 area 0 range 10.0.0.0 255.0.0.0            Same summarization, configured on R2
                                            (the other ABR) — R1 and R4 don't need
                                            to individually know about R3's
                                            10.23.0.0/24 or (via R3's own
                                            summarization) the area 2 network
                                            10.35.0.0/24; a single 10.0.0.0/8
                                            summary is enough.

---------------- Verify (R4) ----------------

Command                Result
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
show ip route            (After allowing a few seconds for reconvergence) R4 now
                         shows a single summarized 10.0.0.0/8 route via OSPF,
                         rather than individual routes to 10.23.0.0/24 and
                         10.35.0.0/24.

==================================================
CONCEPTS LEARNED
==================================================

• Multi-area OSPF and the backbone area (Area 0) — OSPF scales to large
  networks by dividing routers into areas, but every non-backbone area
  must connect directly to Area 0; inter-area traffic must pass through
  the backbone, with no direct area-to-area link allowed to bypass it.
• ABR (Area Border Router) — a router with interfaces in more than one
  OSPF area (R2 and R3 in this lab); ABRs are exactly where route
  summarization between areas is configured, since they're the natural
  boundary point where routes cross from one area into another.
• `network <addr> <wildcard> area <n>` per-interface area assignment —
  each interface's OSPF area is set individually via which `network`
  statement its address falls under; an ABR simply has multiple
  `network` statements pointing at different areas.
• `area <origin-area> range <network> <mask>` — the OSPF inter-area
  route summarization command, configured on an ABR. The area number
  given is the area the routes being summarized are associated with
  FROM THAT ROUTER'S perspective (not necessarily the area the summary
  is being sent into) — and critically, the mask here is a normal
  subnet mask, not a wildcard mask like the `network` command uses,
  which is an easy point of confusion.
• Summarization doesn't affect out-of-range routes — a summary range
  only absorbs routes that actually fall within it; loopback /32
  addresses using a completely different range (e.g. 1.1.1.1/32 against
  a 10.0.0.0/8 summary) continue to be advertised individually, exactly
  as demonstrated in this lab.
• Same OSPF process ID across all routers — while Lab 054 proved
  process IDs don't need to match, this lab uses the same ID (1)
  everywhere, reflecting the more typical real-world convention even
  though it isn't a requirement.

==================================================
STUDY NOTES, EXAM SCENARIOS & REAL-WORLD CONTEXT
==================================================

Every non-backbone area must touch Area 0
  No direct area-to-area link is allowed to bypass the backbone.
  Exam: expect a diagram-based question asking whether a given
  multi-area topology is valid OSPF — the single most common invalid
  design shown is two non-backbone areas connected directly to each
  other with no Area 0 in the path; know to reject that immediately.
  Real world: large enterprise OSPF deployments are deliberately
  designed around this constraint — Area 0 is treated as a dedicated
  "core" that every other area's traffic must transit, which is also
  exactly why summarization on ABRs (as done in this lab) matters so
  much at scale — Area 0 potentially carries traffic between every
  other area combined.

`area <origin> range` vs. the `network` command's wildcard mask
  Same general shape (area/network/mask), but genuinely different mask
  types — a common mix-up.
  Exam: memorize that `area range` uses a normal subnet mask while
  `network` uses a wildcard (inverse) mask — a classic "gotcha" pairing
  exam questions specifically test, precisely because the commands look
  similar enough to blur together under time pressure.
  Real world: this distinction matters in real configuration review too
  — misreading one for the other while auditing a `router ospf` config
  block is an easy, genuinely-made real-world mistake.

Route summarization scaling benefit
  Barely visible in a 5-router lab, but the same principle scales
  dramatically in large networks.
  Exam: know summarization's benefits generally — smaller routing
  tables, less CPU/memory used maintaining them, and (specific to
  link-state protocols like OSPF) fewer LSA floods needed when a
  summarized subnet's individual routes change, since a summary can
  hide small topology changes from other areas entirely.
  Real world: this LSA-flooding-reduction benefit is actually the
  bigger real-world reason to summarize at area boundaries in a large
  OSPF deployment — it isolates topology churn within one area instead
  of forcing every other area to recompute anything.
